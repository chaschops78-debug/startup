# Books in the Mentor's Brain

Structured notes for the entrepreneur-mentor skill. Each entry contains the central thesis, the actionable frameworks, well-known anecdotes that crystallize the ideas, the questions the mentor should ask when invoking the book, and when to reach for it. Notes are written for use by the mentor — they are not substitutes for reading the actual books.

---

## Principles — Ray Dalio

**Central thesis:** Success in any domain comes from systematically discovering, codifying, and applying explicit principles for how to think and act. Treat life and work like engineering problems: identify what works, write it down, test it, refine it.

**Key frameworks:**
- **The 5-step process for getting what you want:** set clear goals → identify the problems blocking them → diagnose root causes → design solutions → execute relentlessly.
- **Pain + Reflection = Progress.** Painful experiences are the highest-bandwidth learning opportunities — but only if you stop and extract the lesson.
- **Radical truth + radical transparency.** Decisions improve when uncomfortable truths are surfaced openly, not buried in politeness.
- **Believability-weighted decision making.** Weight opinions by track record in the relevant domain, not by volume, seniority, or charisma.
- **Idea meritocracy.** The best argument wins, not the highest-ranked person's preference. Requires safety to disagree.
- **"Embrace reality and deal with it."** Wishing the situation were different is the most expensive form of inaction.

**Anecdotes that crystallize the ideas:** Dalio's near-bankruptcy in 1982 after a famously wrong macroeconomic call, which drove him to start codifying principles. Bridgewater's internal "dot collector" system for real-time peer feedback during meetings.

**Mentor questions:**
- What principle would you write down from your last mistake?
- Are you weighting that person's opinion by their track record, or by how loudly they're saying it?
- What's the root cause here, not the symptom?
- What painful reality are you avoiding looking at directly?

**When to invoke:** when the user is repeating mistakes, building a team or culture, making complex multi-factor decisions, or struggling to learn from failure.

## Crossing the Chasm — Geoffrey Moore

**Central thesis:** There's a structural gap between early adopters (who buy disruption) and the early majority (who buy safety and references). Most tech startups die in this chasm because the strategy that wins early adopters doesn't work on the mainstream.

**Key frameworks:**
- **Technology Adoption Lifecycle:** innovators → early adopters → early majority → late majority → laggards.
- **The Chasm:** the gap between early adopters (visionaries seeking advantage) and early majority (pragmatists wanting risk-free productivity).
- **Bowling Alley strategy:** dominate one specific niche segment first. Use that beachhead to knock down adjacent niches. Don't try to win the broad market on day one.
- **Whole Product:** pragmatists buy the entire ecosystem (integrations, references, support, training) — not just the core product.
- **D-Day target customer:** pick one head-pin segment and overwhelm it.

**Anecdotes that crystallize the ideas:** Salesforce starting narrow before expanding horizontally. Documentum focusing entirely on regulated industries before generalizing.

**Mentor questions:**
- Who is your single "head pin" customer segment?
- Are you trying to sell to the early majority with an early-adopter pitch?
- What's the "whole product" your target customer actually needs — and which parts of it don't yet exist?
- What references do they need to see before they'll buy?

**When to invoke:** when the user has early traction but is struggling to scale beyond enthusiasts, when planning go-to-market, when launching into a saturated market.

## The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People — Stephen Covey

**Central thesis:** Lasting effectiveness comes from inside-out, character-based habits rooted in timeless principles — not from techniques or shortcuts.

**The 7 habits:**
1. **Be proactive.** You choose your response. Focus energy on the circle of influence (what you can affect), not the circle of concern (what bothers you but you can't change).
2. **Begin with the end in mind.** Visualize the destination before designing the path. What does success look like 10 years out?
3. **Put first things first.** The Eisenhower matrix: do important-not-urgent work before it becomes urgent. Most failure is failure of prioritization.
4. **Think win-win.** Abundance mentality. Look for outcomes where both parties win, or no deal.
5. **Seek first to understand, then to be understood.** Listen with intent to understand, not to formulate your reply.
6. **Synergize.** Productive collaboration produces results greater than the sum of parts. Different perspectives are an asset, not noise.
7. **Sharpen the saw.** Continuous renewal in physical, mental, social/emotional, and spiritual dimensions. Burnout is a failure of maintenance.

**Mentor questions:**
- Are you spending your energy in the circle of influence or the circle of concern?
- What's the "end in mind" you're building toward — and does this decision serve it?
- Are you operating from win-lose or win-win in this negotiation?
- When did you last sharpen your saw? Are you running on fumes?

**When to invoke:** when the user is overwhelmed, stuck in reactive mode, struggling with prioritization, in interpersonal conflict, or burning out.

## Zero to One — Peter Thiel

**Central thesis:** Real progress comes from going 0 → 1 (creating something new) rather than 1 → n (copying what exists). Monopoly is the goal, not competition. Competition is for losers.

**Key frameworks:**
- **The 7 Questions every business must answer:** Engineering, Timing, Monopoly, People, Distribution, Durability, Secret.
- **The contrarian question:** "What important truth do very few people agree with you on?" Your answer is your secret.
- **Last mover advantage:** be the last mover in a category, not the first. The last great company in a category captures the value.
- **Vertical (new tech) vs horizontal (globalization) progress.**
- **Four types of company** based on monopoly vs commodity dynamics, and how companies disguise their position.
- **Distribution:** technical founders systematically underestimate sales. A great product with bad distribution loses to a mediocre product with great distribution.

**Anecdotes that crystallize the ideas:** Google describing itself as "not a search company" to disguise its search monopoly. PayPal's relentless focus on dominating eBay power-seller payments before going broad. Tesla as a vertical bet on a new transportation stack.

**Mentor questions:**
- What's your secret — what do you believe about this market that few others do?
- Are you starting with a big share of a small market, or chasing scraps of a big one?
- What's your distribution moat?
- Is your "competitive advantage" actually a power, or just "we're better"?

**When to invoke:** when the user is evaluating a new idea, designing market entry, assessing competition, or romanticizing a crowded market.

## The Lean Startup — Eric Ries

**Central thesis:** Startups are experiments operating under extreme uncertainty. The right unit of progress is validated learning, not features shipped or hours worked.

**Key frameworks:**
- **Build-Measure-Learn loop.** Build the smallest thing that tests an assumption → measure customer behavior → learn whether the assumption holds → adjust.
- **Minimum Viable Product (MVP).** The smallest version that lets you test one risky hypothesis. Often not even a product — sometimes a video, a landing page, a manual concierge service.
- **Validated learning.** Progress measured by what you've learned about the customer, not by what you've shipped.
- **Innovation accounting.** Cohort analysis, split tests, actionable metrics over vanity metrics.
- **Pivot vs persevere.** Structured points where you ask: is our theory of the business still right? If not, change one variable (customer, problem, solution, channel).
- **Five Whys.** Repeatedly asking "why?" to find root causes.

**Anecdotes that crystallize the ideas:** Dropbox's MVP video that proved demand before building the product. IMVU's pivot after launch from add-on to standalone. Zappos founder Tony Hsieh photographing shoes at local stores to test online demand before holding inventory.

**Mentor questions:**
- What's the single riskiest assumption your business depends on right now?
- What's the smallest experiment that would test that assumption in under 2 weeks?
- Are you measuring vanity metrics or actionable ones?
- If this assumption is wrong, does your whole business model change?

**When to invoke:** when the user is about to over-build before validating, when growth has stalled, when deciding whether to pivot, when designing a product test.

## Obviously Awesome — April Dunford

**Central thesis:** Positioning is the foundation of marketing — the context that determines whether anything else (messaging, brand, sales) works. Most companies do it accidentally; great ones do it deliberately.

**Key frameworks:**
- **The 5 + 1 components of positioning:**
  1. **Competitive alternatives** — what the customer would use if you didn't exist (often "do nothing" or a spreadsheet).
  2. **Unique attributes** — capabilities only you have.
  3. **Value** — the benefits those attributes deliver to the customer.
  4. **Target market characteristics** — the buyers who care about that value most.
  5. **Market category** — the frame of reference you place yourself in.
  6. **(+1) Trends** — the cultural or market shifts that make your value resonate now.
- **The 10-step positioning process** to systematically diagnose and reset.
- **Reposition rather than rebrand** when growth stalls. Renaming things doesn't fix positioning problems.
- **Match positioning to the buying journey:** different segments need different framing.

**Anecdotes that crystallize the ideas:** Help Scout repositioning from generic "help desk" to focused "customer support for growing businesses." Dunford's own case work on dozens of B2B repositioning projects.

**Mentor questions:**
- What category would your buyer compare you against if you weren't there?
- What unique capabilities do only you have, and what value do those create?
- Are you in the right category, or did a default category get pinned on you?
- Is your problem actually positioning, or is it the product?

**When to invoke:** when the user can't explain their product crisply, when conversion rates are weak despite traffic, when they're being compared to the wrong competitors, when growth has flatlined.

## The Hard Thing About Hard Things — Ben Horowitz

**Central thesis:** There is no formula for the hardest decisions in startups — the ones with no good answers. You build the muscle by facing them, not by reading about them. Most startup advice is for the easy problems.

**Key concepts:**
- **Wartime CEO vs Peacetime CEO.** Different modes for different phases. In peacetime, you expand the market; in wartime, you survive an existential threat. Style and decisions differ.
- **The Struggle.** The existential dread of running a startup that's almost dying. You can't avoid it; you can only walk through it.
- **One management lesson:** do what's right for the company even when it's personally painful — firing your friend, taking a pay cut, demoting a loyal early hire.
- **Don't bullshit your team.** People can tell. Trust is the foundation of execution, and false optimism destroys it faster than bad news.
- **"Take care of the people, the products, and the profits — in that order."**
- **Hire people who outclass you** in their domain.

**Anecdotes that crystallize the ideas:** Horowitz's near-death decisions at Loudcloud/Opsware — selling units of the business mid-meltdown, layoffs the night before earnings, demoting a close friend as Head of Sales. The whole book is essentially a memoir of impossible decisions.

**Mentor questions:**
- What hard truth are you avoiding telling your team?
- Are you in wartime or peacetime — and does your management style match?
- What's the decision you keep delaying because there's no good answer?
- Are you protecting yourself or the company with this choice?

**When to invoke:** when the user faces firing or layoffs, near-death moments, co-founder disputes, or any decision where every option feels bad.

## The Mom Test — Rob Fitzpatrick

**Central thesis:** Customer interviews lie systematically. Even your mom will tell you your business idea is great. The fix is asking questions even your mom couldn't lie about — by anchoring everything in specific past behavior.

**Three rules:**
1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** Don't pitch. Don't explain what you're building.
2. **Ask about specifics from the past, not generic opinions about the future.** Past behavior is real data; hypothetical intent is fantasy.
3. **Talk less, listen more.** Most founders ruin interviews by leading the witness or seeking validation.

**Bad questions:** "Would you buy this?" "Do you think this is a good idea?" "Would you pay X for Y?" — all hypothetical, all flattering, all useless.

**Good questions:** "Tell me about the last time you experienced this problem." "Walk me through how you solved it." "What did you do next?" "How much did that cost you in time and money?"

**Commitments and advancement:** at the end of every meeting, the customer should commit something — time, money, or reputation — or you didn't really get value. Three currencies: time (follow-up meetings, hours of testing), money (deposits, pre-orders, paid pilots), reputation (introductions to others, public endorsement).

**Anecdotes that crystallize the ideas:** Fitzpatrick's own founder stories of misreading verbal enthusiasm as real demand and building products no one bought.

**Mentor questions:**
- What specific past behavior have you observed, vs. verbal interest?
- What did the customer commit to at the end of the meeting — time, money, or reputation?
- Are people currently cobbling together a solution because nothing exists? That's the only signal that matters.
- Did you pitch your idea before listening? That contaminates everything that follows.

**When to invoke:** when the user wants to validate an idea, is designing customer research, or is excited because "everyone I talked to loved it."

## Inside the Tornado — Geoffrey Moore

**Central thesis:** After crossing the chasm into the early majority, markets enter a hypergrowth phase (the "tornado") that requires completely different strategy from the bowling-alley phase that got you there. Most companies misread the transition and lose their window.

**Key concepts:**
- **The full lifecycle:** Early Market → Chasm → Bowling Alley → Tornado → Main Street → End of Life. Each phase has different winning strategies.
- **Tornado strategy:** during hypergrowth, maximize share by shipping a commodity offering as fast as possible. Stop customizing. Stop hand-holding. Volume and speed win.
- **Main Street strategy:** after the tornado settles, market shares stabilize and the game becomes operational excellence plus services-led differentiation.
- **Three competitive roles in a settled market:**
  - **Gorillas** — dominant platform owners. They set the rules and capture most of the value.
  - **Chimps** — proprietary architectures that are profitable but stuck in a niche they can't escape.
  - **Monkeys** — clones and imitators of the gorilla, competing on price.

Different roles require completely different sales, marketing, and product strategies.

**Anecdotes that crystallize the ideas:** Cisco riding the networking tornado in the 1990s. Microsoft riding the PC tornado. SAP riding the enterprise software tornado. In each case, the gorilla emerged because it stopped customizing and started shipping.

**Mentor questions:**
- What phase of the lifecycle is your market in — early market, bowling alley, tornado, or main street?
- Is your current strategy matched to that phase?
- Are you over-customizing during a tornado, when you should be shipping commodity?
- If your market is in tornado mode, what's your share grab plan?

**When to invoke:** when the user has product-market fit and is scaling, when deciding whether to take a custom deal vs. hold the standard, when reading a market that's about to inflect.

## The Gorilla Game — Geoffrey Moore, Paul Johnson, Tom Kippola

**Central thesis:** In discontinuous-innovation tech markets, one company typically becomes the dominant platform owner ("the gorilla") and captures most of the long-term value. The strategic and investing question is how to identify the future gorilla before it's obvious.

**Key concepts:**
- **Discontinuous innovation creates winner-take-most markets.** Continuous improvement does not.
- **Hypergrowth conditions** allow gorillas to emerge — they need a tornado to consolidate power.
- **Gorilla, chimp, monkey roles** (same taxonomy as Inside the Tornado).
- **Power markers** that signal a gorilla candidate:
  - Proprietary open architecture (others build on top of you)
  - High switching costs (customers can't easily leave)
  - Network effects (more users make the platform more valuable)
- **Gorilla candidate criteria:** discontinuous tech, proprietary architecture, mass-market opportunity, hypergrowth dynamics.
- **Investing strategy:** buy a basket of candidates early in the tornado, concentrate as the gorilla emerges, sell chimps quickly because they have a ceiling.

**Anecdotes that crystallize the ideas:** Cisco in networking, Microsoft in operating systems, Oracle in databases — each became the gorilla in a discontinuous market. Apple as a chimp that escaped chimp-hood by re-platforming around iOS.

**Mentor questions:**
- Is this a discontinuous innovation market, or a continuous one? (Only discontinuous markets produce gorillas.)
- Who is the likely gorilla, and what are they doing right?
- If you're entering this market, are you positioned to be a gorilla candidate, a chimp, or a monkey?
- What proprietary architecture or network effect could you build that competitors can't?

**When to invoke:** when the user is evaluating a tech market's structure, considering whether to fight a platform war, or making investment-style bets on emerging tech categories.

## How the mentor uses these books together

The 10 books cover four overlapping concerns:

- **Idea + market evaluation:** Zero to One, Gorilla Game, Crossing the Chasm
- **Customer + product validation:** Mom Test, Lean Startup
- **Growth + positioning:** Crossing the Chasm, Inside the Tornado, Obviously Awesome
- **Leadership + decision-making + self:** Hard Thing About Hard Things, Principles, 7 Habits

The mentor's job is to recognize which concern the user is bringing to him, pull the right book(s), and apply the relevant framework to the user's specific situation — never reciting, always applying.
